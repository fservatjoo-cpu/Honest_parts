DIY GUIDES

Simple jobs you can do yourself in the driveway, explained the way we'd explain it in person — no jargon, no skipped steps. If it's not on this list, ask Mike on the home page.

Checking Your Brake Pads

15 MIN

Squealing when you brake, or just want peace of mind before a road trip.

  1. Park on level ground, engine off, and locate the wheel closest to a brake caliper you can see through the wheel spokes.
  2. Look through the caliper opening at the pad pressed against the rotor — most have a wear groove or metal backing you can spot with a flashlight.
  3. If the pad material looks thinner than about a quarter inch (roughly a pencil eraser's thickness), it's time to replace them soon.
  4. Repeat on the other front wheel — front pads usually wear faster than rear.

If you can't see the pad clearly or you hear grinding, that's worth having a second set of eyes on — ask Mike before you keep driving on it.

Changing Your Wiper Blades

10 MIN

Streaking, skipping, or squeaking every time it rains.

  1. Lift the wiper arm away from the windshield until it locks upright.
  2. Find the small tab or clip where the blade connects to the arm and press it while sliding the old blade down and off.
  3. Slide the new blade on until it clicks into place, matching the connector type to your old blade.
  4. Lower the arm gently back onto the glass — don't let it snap down on its own.

Bring your old blade with you if you're buying in person — connector shapes vary a lot between makes.

Checking Engine Oil Level

5 MIN

Good habit before a long drive, or if the oil light flickered on.

  1. Park on level ground and let the engine sit for a few minutes after driving so oil settles back in the pan.
  2. Pull the dipstick out, wipe it clean with a rag, then push it all the way back in.
  3. Pull it out again and check where the oil film sits against the min/max marks near the tip.
  4. If it's near or below the low mark, top up gradually with the oil type listed in your owner's manual, checking again after each small pour.

Not sure which oil your engine takes? Ask Mike — he'll ask your year, make, and model and tell you.

Jump-Starting a Car Safely

10 MIN

Battery's dead and you've got jumper cables or a jump box handy.

  1. Park the working car close enough for the cables to reach, engines off, both cars in park with parking brakes on.
  2. Connect red to the dead battery's positive terminal, then red to the working battery's positive terminal.
  3. Connect black to the working battery's negative terminal, then black to an unpainted metal bolt or bracket on the dead car's engine — not the dead battery itself.
  4. Start the working car, let it run a couple minutes, then try starting the dead car. Remove cables in reverse order once it's running.

If it won't hold a charge after this, the battery itself is likely the problem — ask Mike to confirm before you buy a new one.

Reading Dashboard Warning Lights

2 MIN

Something lit up on your dash and you're not sure if it's urgent.

  1. Note the color: red usually means stop and check now, yellow/orange means get it looked at soon, green/blue are just status indicators.
  2. Check your owner's manual's symbol chart — glovebox is the usual spot — to match the icon to its meaning.
  3. For a red engine, oil, or temperature light, pull over safely and shut the engine off rather than continuing to drive.
  4. For a yellow check-engine light with no other symptoms, it's usually safe to drive carefully to a shop within a few days.

Don't recognize the symbol or it's flashing? Describe it to Mike and he'll walk you through it.

Checking Your Fluids

10 MIN

Quick monthly check — coolant, brake fluid, and washer fluid.

  1. With the engine cold, find the coolant reservoir (usually a semi-clear plastic tank) and check the level against its min/max lines.
  2. Check the brake fluid reservoir near the back of the engine bay — level should sit between the marks on the side, and the fluid should look clear to light amber, not dark brown.
  3. Top off washer fluid if it's low — it doesn't affect how the car runs, but it's easy to forget until you need it.
  4. Never open the coolant cap on a hot engine — pressure can cause burns.

Brake fluid looking dark or the pedal feeling soft? That's worth asking Mike about before it becomes a bigger issue.

Rotating Your Tires

30 MIN

Evens out tire wear — worth doing roughly every 5,000-7,500 miles.

  1. With the car safely jacked and supported on stands, remove all four wheels.
  2. Move front tires straight back to the rear on the same side, and rear tires forward to the front on the opposite side (front-wheel-drive pattern) — check your manual, since the pattern differs for rear- and all-wheel-drive.
  3. Reinstall each wheel and hand-tighten lug nuts in a star pattern before torquing to spec.
  4. Lower the car and torque the lug nuts again once all four wheels are on the ground.

Not comfortable jacking up your own car? Most shops will do a rotation quickly and cheaply — ask Mike what a fair price looks like.

Battery Care in Cold Weather

5 MIN

Cold weather is when weak batteries finally give out.

  1. Check the battery terminals for white or greenish corrosion buildup, and clean it off with a wire brush if you see any.
  2. Make sure the terminal clamps are snug — a loose connection can mimic a dead battery.
  3. If your battery is 3+ years old, get it load-tested before winter hits rather than waiting for it to fail on a cold morning.
  4. Avoid running accessories (heater, lights, radio) for long periods with the engine off, especially in the cold.

Slow to start even after cleaning the terminals? That's usually the battery itself — ask Mike to confirm before you buy a new one.
